What we do in this session

Take the lesson plan you already wrote and turn it into a presentation that matches it slide by slide.

Session 2 wrote the lesson plan. This session turns it into the slides you will stand in front of. The point is not a beautiful deck — it is a deck that teaches the lesson you planned, in the order you planned it, with the minutes you gave each stage. That only happens if you hand the AI your plan instead of describing your topic to it.

Ask any AI for “a presentation about the present continuous” and you will get a competent deck for somebody else’s lesson. Attach your plan and your book pages, and the same tool builds your lesson: your warm-up, your guided practice, your group task, your exit ticket, and your examples from the pages your class has in front of them.

The eight things you will do today

  1. Open the lesson plan you already wroteThe plan from Session 2, or any plan of your own. This is the spine of the presentation.
  2. Choose the book pages the slides may usePhotograph or scan them. They become the only source of vocabulary, examples and exercises.
  3. Build the presentation prompt22 red fields. Fill them by hand on this page, or let the builder write the whole prompt for you.
  4. Set the number of slides and the minutesTwo numbers decide whether the deck fits your period. Both go into the prompt and both get checked at the end.
  5. Create the deck in GammaPaste the deck text into Gamma, read the outline before it builds, choose a theme, and generate.
  6. Check every slide against your lesson planNine checks. Every stage present, in your order, with your minutes and nothing invented.
  7. Fix the slides the AI got wrongIt merges stages, writes bullets you were meant to read out, and puts garbled words inside generated pictures.
  8. Export the deck and present itA file for the projector, a PDF for the folder, and a link for the students who were absent.

The order, in one line: lesson plan → book pages → prompt → deck text → Gamma → check against the plan → export. Every slide you show should be traceable back to a stage of the plan you wrote before the AI was involved.

Part 1 · Gather the three things the AI needs

The prompt, your lesson plan, and the pages of the book. Nothing else, and none of the three is optional.

  1. The promptThe long instruction that tells the AI what a teaching presentation is: what every slide must carry, what it may take from your pages, and what it must never invent. It is on this page, and the builder writes it for you.
  2. Your lesson planThe spine. Its stages become the sections of the deck, in its order, with its minutes. Attach it as a file — a Word file, a PDF, or a photograph of the paper one your school uses.
  3. The book pagesThe content. The vocabulary, the rule, the reading text and the exercises all come from here, so the slides say what your students’ own book says. One clear photograph per page is enough.

Send all three in one message. The prompt tells the AI how to behave, the plan tells it what order to teach in, and the pages tell it what it is allowed to say. Leave out the plan and it invents a lesson structure. Leave out the pages and it invents examples your class has never seen — and both mistakes are invisible until you are standing in front of the room.

Photographing the book pages. One page per photograph, taken from directly above, with the whole page in the frame and no shadow across the text. Name the files by page number before you upload them — page-42.jpg, page-43.jpg — and write those same page numbers in the prompt. Then, when a slide shows an example, you can find where it came from in seconds.

Part 2 · Build the presentation prompt

22 red fields. Answer them in the builder, or edit them straight into the prompt below.

The red fields are the whole job. Every field you must personalise is shown in red, like this: [[EDIT: Grade or stage]]. Select a red field and it highlights whole, so whatever you type replaces it. Copy takes the entire prompt with your edits inside it; Start over puts the original text back. Nothing outside the red fields needs to change.

The builder — answer the fields, it writes the prompt

This is built into the course and works offline. It asks you the 22 questions the prompt needs, then writes the finished prompt in the language you are reading in. It also holds a cheat sheet on every thinker: select a name and read who he was before you decide whether he belongs in your speaker notes. Pick your route at the top right — Gamma, ChatGPT or Gemini — and the last line of the prompt changes to match.

The design fields are menus, not blank boxes. Choose a pastel palette, a visual style and a table style, and the builder writes the full instruction that sits behind your choice — including the rule that every table gets a coloured header band, alternating pastel rows and a border on all four sides of every cell. That rule is what keeps the deck looking rich after you export it to PDF, where a borderless table collapses into grey text. Every menu ends with Other, which opens a box for your own words.

Choose Arabic and the prompt changes shape. Choose English and the prompt stays English. Choose Arabic and the builder adds the mandatory Arabic font-and-export block.

Or save the master prompt as a file

A prompt this long does not belong pasted into a chat box. Pasted text arrives as a message and competes with everything else in the conversation; a file arrives as a document the AI can go back and re-read while it works. Save it once and attach it to every presentation you ever build, beside your lesson plan and your book pages.

You build presentations a teacher can teach from, not slides that only look good. Your answer will be pasted straight into Gamma to become the finished deck, so it must be clean, complete and in the exact format asked for at the end. --- ## C — CONTEXT - Teacher: [[EDIT: Teacher name]] - Subject: [[EDIT: Subject]] - Grade or stage: [[EDIT: Grade or stage]] - Level or standard: [[EDIT: Level or standard, or write “none”]] - Lesson title: [[EDIT: Lesson title]] - Who is in the room: [[EDIT: Who is in the room]] - Lesson length: [[EDIT: Lesson length — e.g. 30 minutes / 35 minutes / 40 minutes]] minutes - Number of slides: [[EDIT: Number of slides — e.g. 6 slides — a short input lesson / 8 slides / 10 slides — fits a free Gamma generation]] - Language of the presentation: [[EDIT: Language of the presentation — e.g. English / Arabic / Bilingual — Arabic and English]] - Tone: [[EDIT: Tone — e.g. Friendly and encouraging / Warm and playful / Calm and clear]] ### Two files are attached to this message 1. **My lesson plan — [[EDIT: File name of the lesson plan you attached]].** This is the spine of the presentation. Do not reorder it. Do not merge two stages into one slide. Do not add a stage that is not in it. - Take every fact, word, rule, example and exercise from the attached book pages. If a slide needs something that is not on those pages, write `[TEACHER TO ADD]` instead of inventing it. - Do not invent statistics, quotations, research findings, curriculum codes or page numbers. - Where my lesson plan gives a stage a time, put that time on the slide. - If my lesson plan and the book pages disagree, follow the lesson plan and say so at the end under **Questions for the teacher**. --- ## T — TASK ### 1. Map the plan before you write a single slide Produce a mapping table first: one row for every stage of my lesson plan, the minutes it is given, and the slide numbers that carry it. Every stage must appear. Then check the total against the number of slides asked for above. If it does not fit, change how many slides a stage gets — never the order, and never by dropping a stage. ### 2. The presentation must contain these stages [[EDIT: Keep only the stages your lesson plan actually contains]] - a warm-up that activates what the class already knows - a learning-objectives slide written with the verbs of my lesson plan - the explanation, broken into small pieces with an example before every rule - guided practice done with me, including a worked example and the expected answers - an individual task with clear instructions, a time and a success criterion - a group task with roles, a product and a time - free practice on a new example, not a copy of the guided one - a quick check of understanding tied to the objective - homework that extends the lesson instead of repeating it - a closing reflection or exit ticket Do not add an activity my lesson plan does not include. ### 3. Every single slide carries all of this - **Title** — short, no more than eight words. - **Bullets** — three to five, one idea each, no more than twelve words each, sentence case, no full stop at the end. - **Speaker notes** — one or two sentences that say what I should actually say, ending with a sentence that leads into the next slide. - **Visual** — describe one specific visual. Never write "add an image". Say what it shows, who is in it and what the learner should look at. Choose from: photograph, educational illustration, diagram, timeline, flowchart, icon set, comparison table, map, infographic, chart, classroom scene. - **Layout** — one practical instruction: image left with text right, full-width visual with a text overlay, two-column comparison, one large central diagram, three cards, a timeline across the slide, or title on top with the activity panel below. - **Stage and time** — which stage of my lesson plan this slide belongs to, and its minutes. - **Activity block, on activity slides only** — the activity name, the instructions, the time, the materials, what the student produces, and the success criterion the student can check for themselves. ### 4. Data and charts Where the attached book pages contain numbers worth showing, name the chart type, say what it must show, and leave a placeholder. Bar charts for comparisons, line charts for change over time, pie or donut only for a simple proportion, timelines for sequences, flowcharts for processes, tables for exact values. Do not invent data. ### 5. Design — rich, colourful, and still readable once it is a PDF - Colour palette: [[EDIT: Colour palette — e.g. Pastel rainbow — a tint per section / Cool pastels — mint, sky, lilac / Warm pastels — peach, apricot, cream]] - Fonts: [[EDIT: Fonts — e.g. Accessible — rounded headings, plain body / One clean modern sans / Serif headings, sans body]] - Logo: [[EDIT: Logo — e.g. Top-right, every slide / Top-left, every slide / Bottom-right, every slide]] - Visual style: [[EDIT: Visual style — e.g. Rich and colourful — pastel panels / Clean and minimal / Playful and child-friendly]] - Image style: [[EDIT: Image style — e.g. Real photographs / Flat vector illustration / Hand-drawn storybook]] - Tables and frames: [[EDIT: Tables and frames — e.g. Pastel fill, full frame border / Pastel header, full outer frame / White cells, full frame border]] **Build a small colour system and hold to it.** Give each stage of my lesson plan its own tint from the palette above, and use that one tint for that stage's panel fill, its heading rule, its section tab and its table header — so a learner can tell from the colour alone which part of the lesson they are in. List the colours you settled on at the end of the deck text, with a name for each, so I can rebuild them. **Contrast is not a matter of taste.** Dark ink on a pastel fill. Never white text on a pastel fill. Never text laid directly over a busy photograph — put it on a solid or lightly tinted panel first. **Whenever a table appears — and only use one when the content is genuinely tabular — frame it fully.** A border on every side of every cell, a filled header row in a deeper tint of that section's colour, alternating pastel rows beneath it, generous padding inside the cells, no more than five columns, and a one-line caption above the table saying what it shows. Never a borderless table, and never a table used only to line text up. **Panels, boxes and callouts get the same treatment:** a pastel fill, a full border on all four sides, and a rounded corner. An activity box, a definition box and a "check yourself" box should each be recognisable by colour before the learner reads a word of them. **It will be exported to PDF and printed, so:** solid fills rather than gradients, borders no thinner than one point, no pale text on a pale ground, and nothing that depends on a hover or an animation to be understood. **Read this only if the presentation language above is Arabic, or Arabic and English together. If the presentation is in English only, ignore it.** **This block replaces every other font, direction, numeral, layout and export instruction anywhere in this prompt. Where it disagrees with anything above, it wins.** ### الخط العربي والتصدير — متطلبات إلزامية - استخدم خط Arial فقط في العرض كاملًا - استخدم Arial Bold لجميع العناوين - استخدم Arial Regular لجميع النصوص - طبِّق Arial على خط العناوين وخط النص داخل Theme - لا تستخدم Arial Abjadhaus أو Noto Sans Arabic - لا تستخدم خطوطًا زخرفية أو غير متوفرة في PowerPoint - لا تخلط بين أكثر من عائلة خطوط داخل العرض - اجعل اتجاه جميع النصوص العربية من اليمين إلى اليسار RTL - اجعل محاذاة النصوص والقوائم والجداول إلى اليمين - حافظ على اتصال الحروف العربية وترتيب الكلمات الصحيح - استخدم الأرقام العربية الهندية فقط: ٠، ١، ٢، ٣، ٤، ٥، ٦، ٧، ٨، ٩ - تجنب مزج الكلمات الإنجليزية والعربية في السطر نفسه - لا تستخدم تأثيرات أو تدرجات على النصوص العربية - لا تحوِّل المحتوى العربي إلى Timeline أو Smart Diagram - لا تستخدم مخططات Gamma الذكية للنصوص العربية - استخدم صناديق نص وبطاقات وجداول عادية فقط - تأكد من ظهور النص العربي متصلًا في PDF وPowerPoint - راجع شريحة تجريبية بعد التصدير قبل تصدير العرض كاملًا ### 6. Accessibility, because it will be read from the back of the room Strong visual hierarchy, large readable type, high contrast, no crowded slides, and visuals that carry meaning rather than decorate. A long explanation belongs in the speaker notes, never on the slide. The deck must still read on a phone screen. ### 7. Must include [[EDIT: A fact, definition, word list or curriculum point that must appear — or “none”]] ### 8. Theory, in the speaker notes only [[EDIT: Thinkers to reference in the speaker notes, or write “none”]] Where my lesson plan already does something one of these thinkers would recognise, add one short line in the **speaker notes** naming it and why the step works. Never on the slide itself, and never forced — if it does not fit, leave it out. --- ## F — FORMAT Return plain Markdown, in exactly this order, and nothing else. No greeting, no explanation before it, no comment after it. ``` ===MAP=== | Lesson plan stage | Minutes | Slides | | --- | --- | --- | | ... | ... | ... | ===OUTLINE=== 1. [Slide title — purpose in six words] 2. [Slide title — purpose in six words] (continue until every slide is listed) ===SLIDES=== ### Slide 1 — [Title] **Stage:** [stage of my lesson plan] · **Time:** [minutes] **Bullets:** - [bullet] - [bullet] - [bullet] **Speaker notes:** [one or two sentences, ending with the transition] **Visual:** [specific description] **Layout:** [layout instruction] **Activity:** [name · instructions · time · materials · student product · success criterion] (repeat the same structure for every slide) ===QUESTIONS FOR THE TEACHER=== - [anything a slide needed that was not on the attached pages] ``` --- ## BEFORE YOU ANSWER — check these silently, then fix what fails 1. Every stage of my lesson plan appears in `===MAP===`, in its original order. 2. The number of slides matches the number asked for above. 3. The minutes across the slides add up to the lesson length above. 4. One idea per slide, and no bullet longer than twelve words. 5. Every slide has speaker notes and a described visual — no slide says "add an image". 6. Nothing appears that is not on the attached book pages, and anything missing is marked `[TEACHER TO ADD]`. 7. No invented data, quotations, research or curriculum codes. 8. Guided practice comes before the individual task, and the individual task before the free practice. 9. The final slide gives the class one clear next step: [[EDIT: The exact next step you want the class to take]] Now build the presentation.

Part 3 · Create the deck in Gamma

Route A — you write the deck text with the AI, then Gamma designs it.

Gamma is not a chatbot with slides attached. It is a design engine: you give it the words and the structure, and it lays out the cards, chooses the type sizes and finds the images. That division of labour is why this route gives you the most control — the teaching decisions are made in the deck text you already checked, and Gamma is only allowed to make the visual ones.

  1. Run the prompt in ChatGPT or Gemini firstAttach the master prompt, your lesson plan and your book pages in one message. What comes back is the deck text: a mapping table, an outline, and every slide written out with its bullets, speaker notes, visual and layout. Read the mapping table before you go anywhere near Gamma — if a stage of your plan is missing, fix it here, in the text, where fixing is cheap.
  2. Sign in at gamma.app and select NewThere is nothing to install. Gamma offers you four ways to start: generate from a prompt, paste in text, start from a template, or import a file or a link.
  3. Choose “Paste in text”, not “Generate”This is the step that decides whether the deck is yours. “Generate” asks Gamma to invent the content from a short description. “Paste in text” tells it to lay out the words you already wrote and checked. Paste the whole deck text — the outline and all the slides.
  4. Set it to a presentation, and set the numbersGamma can also make a document, a web page or a social post — choose presentation. Set the number of cards to the number of slides in your prompt, and choose 16:9 for a data projector. Fluid is for reading on a screen, not for a classroom wall.
  5. Read the outline Gamma shows you before it buildsGamma pauses and shows you the card titles it is about to create. This is the cheapest moment in the whole session to catch a problem: check that every stage of your lesson plan has a card and that they are in your order. Edit titles here, add a card, delete one. Fixing the outline takes a minute; fixing forty finished slides does not.
  6. Choose how much text, which theme, and where images come fromText density: concise, for a class — the detail lives in the speaker notes. Theme: pick one and keep it for every deck you build this term, so your students learn to read your slides. Images: AI-generated, stock photographs, or none at all. For a language or a science lesson, stock photographs of real things usually beat generated art.
  7. Generate, then edit by selecting anything on the cardThe deck builds in front of you. Every piece of text and every picture is editable in place — select it and type. You can also change the theme after the deck exists, so do not spend time on the look until the teaching is right.
  8. Then run the nine checksA finished-looking deck is not a finished deck. Part 5 is the list, and it is not optional.

    Go to the nine checks

Ask for a number of cards you can actually get. A free Gamma account caps how many cards one generation may produce — ten, at the time of writing — and every generation spends credits from your account. So set the slide count in your prompt to a number your plan can live inside. If your lesson genuinely needs more, build it as two decks, one for the first half of the lesson and one for the second, and check the card limit on your own plan before you promise yourself a twenty-slide deck.

Part 5 · The thinkers behind your slides

Select a name and read the cheat sheet: who he was, his core idea, what it looks like in a classroom, and what it changes on a slide.

The prompt lets you name educational thinkers, and the builder lets you tick them. Before you do, read what each one actually said — a theorist named on a slide he would not recognise costs you more credibility than leaving the theory out. The first six names are the ones teachers meet in training. The last two are here for a different reason: Richard Mayer and John Sweller are the only names on this list whose research is about the screen you are building.

Cheat sheet · 1896–1934

Lev Vygotsky

Learning happens with help before it happens alone

Who he was
A Soviet psychologist who died at 37 and whose work only reached the West in the 1960s. He studied how children think when an adult or a stronger classmate is beside them.
The core idea
The zone of proximal development: the distance between what a learner can do alone and what the same learner can do with support. Teaching aims at that gap, not below it and not far above it. Support is temporary and is withdrawn as the learner takes over — what later writers called scaffolding.
In the classroom
Model it, then do it together, then let them do it alone. Talk comes before writing: pair discussion is not a break from the lesson, it is where the thinking is built.
On your slides
Guided practice must have its own slide, and it must come before the individual task. Put the worked example on the slide, not in your head. A group slide needs roles and a product, or the talk has nothing to build.

Cheat sheet · 1896–1980

Jean Piaget

The example has to arrive before the rule

Who he was
A Swiss psychologist who spent decades watching children reason — including his own three — and asking them why they answered as they did.
The core idea
Children are not small adults; they build knowledge in stages, fitting new experience into what they already have and reshaping their thinking when it will not fit. The mismatch is what drives learning. Most primary and lower-secondary learners reason from concrete cases before they can reason from abstract rules.
In the classroom
Give the concrete case first and let the rule be discovered from it. A definition handed over before any example is a sentence to memorise, not an idea to use.
On your slides
Order the explanation slides example → pattern → rule, never rule → example. One new idea per slide. For a class of eleven-year-olds a labelled diagram will do more work than a definition.

Cheat sheet · 1913–1999

Benjamin Bloom

The verb in your objective decides the task

Who he was
An American educational psychologist who chaired the committee that classified educational objectives in 1956, and who later argued that almost all learners can reach mastery if they are given the time and the feedback.
The core idea
Thinking tasks are not all the same size. Remember, understand, apply, analyse, evaluate, create — in the 2001 revision by Anderson and Krathwohl. An objective that says “understand” cannot be assessed by a task that only asks the class to remember.
In the classroom
Write the objective with the verb you will actually assess, then build the task from that verb. Ladder your questions upward instead of asking eight questions from the same level.
On your slides
The objectives slide should carry the same verbs as your lesson plan, word for word. On the check-for-understanding slide, put the questions in rising order so the class can feel the climb.

Cheat sheet · 1925–2021

Albert Bandura

They copy what they watch, then believe they can do it

Who he was
A Canadian-American psychologist at Stanford, best known for the Bobo doll experiments and for later putting the learner's own belief in their ability at the centre of his theory.
The core idea
Much learning happens by watching someone else do it and seeing what follows — no direct reward to the learner required. And self-efficacy, the learner's judgement of whether they can succeed, predicts how long they will persist when the work gets hard.
In the classroom
Demonstrate before you instruct. Let the class watch a classmate at their own level succeed, not only the teacher — a peer model is more convincing than an expert one. Break the skill into steps small enough to be finished.
On your slides
Add a worked-example slide the class watches before they try. Give every activity a success criterion written so a student can check it themselves — that is what turns finishing into confidence.

Cheat sheet · b. 1941

Stephen Krashen

Understandable language, just above where they are

Who he was
An American linguist at the University of Southern California whose account of second-language acquisition has shaped language teaching since the 1980s, and is still argued about.
The core idea
Language is acquired by understanding messages slightly beyond the learner's current level — what he wrote as i+1 — not by studying rules about it. And anxiety works like a filter: a learner who is afraid of being wrong stops taking the language in.
In the classroom
Fill the lesson with language they can almost understand, and let meaning come before form. Lower the stakes early so the quiet ones will speak.
On your slides
Pitch the slide language a little above the class, and let the picture carry the meaning so the text can stay short. Do not open on a grammar rule — open on something they can understand and want to answer.

Cheat sheet · b. 1946

Daniel Goleman

A class that feels safe is a class that answers

Who he was
An American psychologist and science journalist whose 1995 book put emotional intelligence into ordinary language and into school policy.
The core idea
Recognising and handling emotion — your own and other people's — is a set of learnable skills, not a fixed trait: self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skill. It draws on Salovey and Mayer's earlier research, and the strong claims about it are still debated.
In the classroom
Name feelings out loud, teach turn-taking as a skill, and make being wrong survivable. Thirty seconds of settling at the start buys more than it costs.
On your slides
Let the opening slide lower the stakes rather than test them. On a group slide, write down how to disagree, not only what to produce. End with a reflection slide the class answers for itself.

Cheat sheet · b. 1947

Richard Mayer

The research that is actually about slides

Who he was
An American educational psychologist at UC Santa Barbara who has spent thirty years running experiments on how people learn from words and pictures together. If you present anything on a screen, this is your evidence base.
The core idea
The cognitive theory of multimedia learning: people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone — but only when the extra material is removed, the label sits next to the part it names, and the same words are not delivered twice at once. Reading your own bullet points aloud makes learning worse, not better.
In the classroom
Say more than the screen says. The screen is the caption; your voice is the explanation. Cut the decorative photograph, the background music and the animation that means nothing.
On your slides
Never write a sentence you intend to read out. Put each label on the diagram beside the thing it names, not in a legend underneath. Every image must do a job — if you cannot say what it teaches, delete it.

Cheat sheet · b. 1946

John Sweller

Working memory is small; one idea per slide

Who he was
An Australian educational psychologist at the University of New South Wales who built cognitive load theory out of experiments on problem solving in the 1980s.
The core idea
Working memory holds only a few new items at a time. Some of the load is the material itself and cannot be removed; the rest comes from how it is presented, and that part is your responsibility. Splitting a learner's attention between a diagram and a paragraph somewhere else costs them the lesson.
In the classroom
Give beginners the worked example before the practice, not the practice first. As they get better, take the support away — the same help that carried a beginner slows an expert down.
On your slides
One idea per slide, and split a heavy slide into two rather than shrinking the type. Keep a diagram and its labels on the same slide. Three to five short bullets, because a sixth one is not read.

Theory goes in the speaker notes, never on the slide. A class of eleven-year-olds does not need to see the word “scaffolding”; they need the guided practice that scaffolding describes. Name the thinker for yourself, in the notes, as a reason the step is there — so that when a colleague or an inspector asks why your lesson is shaped this way, the answer is already written down. The master prompt enforces this, and so should you.

Put your lesson plan on one side of the screen and the deck on the other, and work down this list. It takes about five minutes, and it is the difference between a deck that looks professional and a deck that teaches your lesson.

  • Every stage of your lesson plan has at least one slide, and the stages are in your order.
  • The number of slides is the number you asked for.
  • The minutes written on the slides add up to the length of your period.
  • Nothing appears that is not on the book pages you attached — and whatever was missing is marked, not invented.
  • No slide says “add an image”. Every visual is described, and every picture on the finished slide does a job you can name.
  • Every slide has speaker notes, and no bullet is a sentence you were planning to read out.
  • Guided practice comes before the individual task, and the individual task before the free practice.
  • Every activity has a time, a product the student hands in, and a success criterion the student can check alone.
  • The last slide gives the class one clear next step, not a thank-you.

What the first generation usually gets wrong

This is not a warning about bad tools. These four things happen with good prompts and good tools, and the only reason you will catch them is that you are looking for them.

  • It marries two stages into one slideThe warm-up and the objectives are the usual pair, and the exit ticket gets folded into the summary. Split them back apart — a stage without its own slide will be skipped in the room.
  • It writes bullets you were meant to say out loudFull sentences on the slide mean you and the screen are competing to say the same thing. Cut each one back to a caption of a few words and move the sentence into the speaker notes.
  • It slips in an example your book pages do not containUsually a good example, which is what makes it dangerous. If it is not on the pages you attached, either delete it or say in the lesson that it is yours, not the book’s.
  • Generated pictures come back with broken words inside themAI image tools still cannot spell reliably inside a picture. Choose an image with no writing in it and put your words on top as real text, where you can correct them and a screen reader can find them.

Part 7 · Edit, export and present

The last twenty minutes, and the ones that decide what the class actually sees.

  1. Fix the slides the checks caughtSplit the merged stage, cut the read-aloud bullets, delete or credit the invented example, replace the picture with broken writing in it. Do the teaching fixes before any design fixes.
  2. Put your school’s identity on itYour theme, your colours, your logo in the same corner on every slide. Set it once in the theme rather than pasting the logo onto each card.
  3. Read it from the back of your own classroomOpen the deck on the projector you will actually use and walk to the back wall. Type that is comfortable on a laptop disappears at four metres, and a low-contrast theme that looked elegant at your desk becomes unreadable under classroom lights.
  4. Export what you need, and check the exportA PowerPoint file if your school requires .pptx, a PDF for the lesson folder, and the link for students who were absent. Then open the exported file and look for three things: that the layouts survived, that the table borders and the pastel fills are still there, and that your speaker notes came with it. Notes are the part that most often does not travel. On an Arabic deck, do one thing before you export anything else: export a single test slide, open it, and look at whether the letters are still joined and still running right to left. Check it in the PDF and in the PowerPoint file, because they can fail differently. One test slide costs a minute; a whole deck exported wrong costs the lesson.
  5. Teach from the notes, not from the screenThe slide is the caption; you are the explanation. If you find yourself reading the bullets to the class, the slide has too many words on it — and you now know exactly which line to cut before next lesson.
